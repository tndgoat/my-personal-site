There's a particular kind of tiredness that sleep doesn't fix. Not physical exhaustion — that kind you can account for, treat, recover from. I mean the tiredness that accumulates from moving too fast through your own life. From consuming the summary instead of the book, the highlight reel instead of the conversation, the metric instead of the experience. You're present, technically. But you're somewhere else mentally — already at the next thing.

I've lived in that mode for long stretches. I suspect most people who think seriously about productivity have.

## The Paradox We Refuse to See

Modern productivity culture has a strange relationship with time. The framing is always scarcity — not enough hours, too many obligations, inboxes that refill while you sleep. The solution it offers is optimization: batch your tasks, eliminate friction, systemize your mornings. Do more in less time so you can... do more.

It rarely asks what the *more* is for.

There's a philosophical problem buried here that optimization glosses over: if speed is the value, there's no natural stopping point. You can always be faster. You can always clear one more thing. The efficiency mindset colonizes leisure the same way it colonizes work — suddenly your walk needs a podcast, your vacation needs a schedule, your friendship needs an agenda.

The philosopher Byung-Chul Han calls this the "achievement society" — a culture where the greatest violence isn't external coercion but self-imposed acceleration. We don't need anyone to push us anymore. We do it ourselves, and call it ambition.

## What Slowness Actually Feels Like

Here's what I've noticed, and it surprised me when I first admitted it: slowing down doesn't feel relaxing, at least not at first. It feels uncomfortable. When I've deliberately removed the stimulus — no phone on the walk, no background podcast, no task to optimize — there's an initial restlessness that's hard to sit with.

That restlessness is information. It's pointing at something: an anxiety about being unproductive, a fear that the world is moving while I'm standing still, a discomfort with thoughts that don't go anywhere useful. The speed was partly functioning as avoidance.

The clarity that eventually arrives — and it does arrive, given enough patience — isn't mystical. It's just what happens when your attention isn't shattered across seventeen threads simultaneously. A problem you've been stuck on resolves itself. A relationship you've been neglecting surfaces in your mind. A want that you'd been too busy to notice makes itself known.

## Where I Keep Getting Stuck

I don't want to write this post as if I've figured it out. I haven't. The honest version is that I find intentional slowness genuinely difficult to sustain. Not because I don't value it — I do, more than I once did — but because the environment is architected against it.

The counterintuitive thing is that slowness requires *active maintenance* in a way that speed does not. Speed is the default. The inbox fills on its own. The notification arrives without invitation. Choosing slowness is a repeated, effortful decision — which creates its own irony: you have to be somewhat disciplined and intentional to be slow. You can't just let it happen.

I'm not sure what to do with that yet. It suggests that "slow living" as a lifestyle prescription misses something — you can't simply decide to decelerate and sustain it without structural changes that most people aren't positioned to make. The monk on the mountain isn't slow because of superior character. He built a life where slowness is the path of least resistance. Most of us haven't.

## A Practice That Actually Works (for me, sometimes)

I'm skeptical of universal prescriptions, but this one is concrete enough to be testable: **deliberately finish one thing before starting the next**.

Not forever. Not as a philosophy. Just for a day, or an afternoon. Read one article entirely before opening another tab. Eat one meal without a screen. Have one conversation where you're not also composing a reply in your head.

What I notice, when I actually do this, is that the quality of experience changes. Not the content — I might be reading something mundane or eating something ordinary. But there's a dimensionality to it that disappears in the fragmented version. Things have texture again. Time feels less like a resource being spent and more like a medium being inhabited.

That's the closest I can get to describing what "slowing down" means to me, beyond the platitudes. It's not about doing less. It's about being somewhere, actually, rather than somewhere else mentally while your body goes through the motions.

## The Open Question

I keep returning to something I can't resolve: is the desire for slowness itself a kind of privilege? The people I know who don't worry about pace are either very wealthy or very poor — one group has bought their way out of urgency, the other has never had the luxury of optimizing. The middle stretch, where most people live, is where the acceleration is most intense and most coerced.

So when I advocate for slowing down — even gently, even to myself — I'm aware that it's easier advice to take from some positions than others. That doesn't make it wrong. But it means the conversation about speed and presence is incomplete if it stays at the individual level. The problem isn't just attention management. It's also structural.

I don't have a clean answer. That's probably the honest place to end.
